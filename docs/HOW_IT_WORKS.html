Epic Game Pass When? / Plain-language guide

How the Predictor Thinks

What the site is doing when it tells you a game will be free in fourteen months, how it decides what kind of answer to give, and how much weight each answer can bear.

Data as of 25 September 2026 · updated quarterly · Companion to the Arrival Model Handbook

The question it answers

You give it a game and a service. It tells you when that game is likely to show up free there — or, when a date would not be honest, how likely it is to show up at all.

That is the whole job. Not whether a game is good, not whether you should buy it — just when, and how sure.

Humble Choice picks games up in about a year and a half. Epic takes closer to three and a half years. One predictor averaging them together would be wrong for both.

How it decides what kind of answer to give

Not every question needs a forecast. The site asks a few questions in order and stops at the first one that settles it.

01

Is it already there, or officially announced?

For Game Pass and PS Plus, the catalogue itself is the answer. If a game was on the service at the last data update, the site says so — and says it as of that date, because a game can leave between updates. If a departure or an arrival has been officially dated, you get that date.

02

Does the publisher's own policy answer it?

Microsoft's own studios launch on Game Pass on day one. Sony's own games reach PS Plus Extra a year or more after release. New Call of Duty releases join Game Pass about a year after launch. Where a published policy exists, that beats any forecast.

03

Has this game been on the service before?

Then its own history decides — and history says returns are rare. About 1 in 100 Epic giveaways has ever been repeated, about 1 in 14 Game Pass games has ever come back, and about 1 in 8 on PS Plus. So a past appearance usually means "unlikely to return", not "due again".

04

Otherwise, a forecast

For everything else, the predictor for that service estimates when the game is likely to arrive. This is where most new releases land.

What the forecast pays attention to

Mostly the publisher's track record on that service. If a studio's games have historically taken about two years to arrive, a new game from that studio starts from about two years. Review scores and release timing then adjust it.

A publisher with only one or two games on record does not get to set its own pattern — one data point is not a trend — so its estimate leans on the service-wide average instead. A publisher never seen before relies on that average entirely, and the range widens to say so.

Dates, ranges and likelihoods

A forecast comes as a best guess with a range around it — for example, most likely December 2028, somewhere between March 2027 and May 2032. The wording softens as the range widens: most likely, then best estimate, then rough estimate.

When a game is past its usual window

Sometimes the expected date has already gone by. Rather than keep saying "any time now", the site switches to a likelihood, measured from the data: of all the games that old that are not on the service yet, how many actually arrive within the following year.

That likelihood differs a lot by service. A new release not yet on Game Pass has roughly a 1 in 7 chance of arriving within a year; by five years old that falls to about 1 in 35. PS Plus keeps picking up older games at a steady 4–5% a year. Humble rarely takes a game older than four years. Epic sits at 1–2% at every age, simply because it gives away far fewer games than it could.

So a game past its window gets one of three answers, depending on those odds: could be any time now, possible but fading, or unlikely soon — with the actual chance shown alongside.

What "80% of the time" means

A range comes with a promise: the real date will fall inside it about 80% of the time. Whether that promise is kept can be checked simply by counting.

Think of a delivery window. The courier says "Tuesday to Friday". Say that a hundred times, and if the package turns up inside the window eighty of those times, the promise is kept.

A higher number here is not automatically better. The goal is to keep the promise, not to exceed it.

A courier could promise "sometime between January and December" and never be wrong — and you would learn nothing. A good range is both honest and narrow enough to plan around, and those two pull against each other. That is why wide ranges are labelled as rough rather than hidden.

How the data stays current

Game Pass and PS Plus history comes from community-maintained catalogue lists; Epic giveaways and Humble Choice bundles come from their published weekly and monthly line-ups. Everything is refreshed quarterly.

Every answer is only as current as the last update, so the site says when that was — on each result and on the About page, along with when the next update is due. Anything that changed on a service after that date is not reflected until the next refresh, which is why "on Game Pass" is always stated as of our last update rather than as "right now".
